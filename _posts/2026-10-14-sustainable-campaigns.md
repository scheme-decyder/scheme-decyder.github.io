I've been a part of countless Mordheim campaign, where I've painted my warband and been excited for the campaign, only for the campaign to die after two sessions. I wanted to run a [Stargrave](https://www.ospreypublishing.com/us/stargrave-9781472837509/) Campaign, I didn't want it to die, but I also didn't have a huge amount of time to dedicate to managing it. I wanted the campaign to run with little input from me. I was willing to put in up front effort to get the campaign running, but I wanted the ongoing effort to be minimal. This involved putting as much responsibility onto the players as I could.

Players would be responsible for:
* Scheduling games
* Playing games in their own house
* Posting their results

The core concept is that I would post fixtures at the start of the month and everyone would have a month to organise, play and post results. One game per month, six players total. This meant we could have 5 games, each one against a different player, without the number of players being excessive.

I think there are a few rules that the miniature community can borrow from the RPG sphere, which specialises in long running campaigns. The top tips I've found are:
* Get reliable people. Reliability should be valued more than skill at a game, painting ability or similar.
* A Fixed number of sessions. It's a lot easier for people to commit, and keeps them motivated when they know there's only a few sessions left. Indefinite campaigns can leave players demotivated as there's no end in sight. If everyone is still keen at the end another fixed length campaign with the same characters/warbands can always be started.
* Regular sessions. There's nothing more momentum stealing than inconstancy.
* Reward the things you want to see. More on this below.

A key part of RPG campaign design is rewarding what you want to see. If you want tactical fights, then give out experience for killing monsters, if you want an story focussed game, then give milestones when players interact with other characters. I wanted a narrative focussed league, where everyone came up with names stories about all their characters. Therefore, I designed a "League points" system, which rewarded writing stories and taking photos. Each game points were allocated for:
* Playing a game - 1 points
* Gathering loot during a game - 1 point per loot
* Achieving secret objective from Side Hustle- 1 point
* Posting a short 1 paragraph report/story of the game - 3 points
* Posting a photo (or map/drawing of you forgot a photo) - 1 point
As you can see, the points heavily incentivise posting a post game story, much more than doing well at a game. It should be noted that there were no points for explicitly winning. In Stargrave you technically win by getting more loot than your opponent. However, Stargrave encourages it to be up to the players as to whether they think they won or not, rather than an assigned winner. Outcomes are rarely black and white. Points are still assigned for gathering loot and achieving objectives, to give some award for player skill. Due to the number of points awarded for posting stories and photos, the highest players were those playing games, posting stories and posting photos every week, with loot and game outcomes acting more as a tie-breaker.

So what did those League points get you? The idea was at the end of the campaign we would get everyone together for a day long mega game. There would be two teams, with the two highest players on the leaderboard being the 'team captains'. The captains would be able to choose the other players on their team, and would be able to field their whole warband. Other team members would be restricted to a subset of their warband to prevent the game from taking too long. Unfortunately this didn't pan out. I've got a reflection on the campaign towards the end of this post. 

As it was a narrative campaign, I wanted everyone to have painted models. It was a part of the buy in to the campaign that your models would be painted, and I made sure everyone had enough warning before the campaign started.

One of the most challenging things for getting people to play their own games is getting enough terrain for everyone. The good thing about a game like Stargrave is that the terrain is very flexible. Have futuristic ruins? You're on a wrecked city planet! Lots of swamp fantasy terrain? You're on a jungle planet! Houses for a game like Mordheim? You're on a Feudal planet! It can also be played on a 2"x2" or 3"x3" board, so it can be played on the dining table if a player doesn't have a dedicated gaming setup in their house. We are lucky that everyone playing had access to painted terrain, or we had enough were able to loan it to them. There was one player who didn't, who always played 'away'.

Another cool part of Stargrave are the random encounters. These are NPC monsters that wander on the board and attack using a primitive monster AI. There's a lot of monsters in the Stargrave book, which can make it difficult to represent all the monsters. I encouraged all the players to create an encounter table from painted miniatures they had available. The good thing about Alien creatures is that you don't know what they look like so it's easy to find a miniature that can represent it. This is another aspect of the 'use what you've got' aspect of Stargrave that I like.

[![Some terrain in use]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/image/sustainable-campaigns/action_shot.jpg)]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/image/sustainable-campaigns/action_shot.jpg){:.glightbox}

We only used the rules from the core book, apart from the [Side Hustle](https://www.ospreypublishing.com/ca/stargrave-side-hustle-9781472856180/) expansion. This is a deck of cards that contains a random secret mission for players to achieve during the game. The catch is that you need dedicated objective markers to represent the secret missions. The objective markers are one thing I did put effort into, I provided players with painted objectives as I though it may been hard for to find appropriate models that fit on the right bases. I also wanted all the objectives to be consistent between games to prevent confusion at the tabletop. Below you can see my 'pylons', 'pits' and 'destroyed robots', along with physical and data loot tokens. I would 100% recommend the Side Hustle expansion, it added a ton of narrative to all the games, and helped contribute to the 'you decide if you're a winner' feel to the games.

So in the end, what did I have to do each month, outside of the normal player responsibilities? I had to:
* Post the month's pairings (I generated all of these at the start of the campaign)
* Take a photo of everyone's side hustle card and send it to them
* Remind everyone to play their games around mid-month
* Remind everyone to post their stories & photos end of month
That's it! Not a huge amount of work.

That's a lot of talking about what I wanted to happen, but how did the campaign actually go? Quite well in my opinion, with one major caveat. Out of 25 games there was only one that was completely missed, however one or two times we had to extend everyone's allowance by a month. Almost all games had stories and photos posted about them by both players, and people seemed to be enjoying and participating. It was all going well until we got to the end of the campaign and it came to organise the all player mega game. Even there was a poll with options months in advance, there weren't any days that all days were free. Polls were re-posted, but again no days were free, until the game faded away from everyone's memories. If I was to do this again, I would either not have this mega game, or be willing to run it even if only 75% of people could attend.

Although we had to extend a few of the game allowances towards the end of the campaign, people were still keen to keep on playing. They were sad that they would have to leave their warband and wanted to play more than five games.  Is five sessions significantly longer than the most Mordheim campaigns last? Maybe not significantly. I would be interested in how these principles scale to 10 or more game campaigns, or for more players.
